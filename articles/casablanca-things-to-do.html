Casablanca

Casablanca Things to Do: Hassan II Mosque, Architecture & Food (2026)

By Amine Berrada & TravelMoveMorocco Team
Updated March 2026
10 min read (1,610 words)
[ Insert Featured Image: Hassan II Mosque & Atlantic Ocean Esplanade ]

Too many international travelers treat Casablanca (known universally to locals as "Casa") merely as an unavoidable transit hub—a fleeting glimpse through an airport taxi window on the way to Marrakech or Fes. To dismiss it so quickly is to miss Morocco's pulsating, cosmopolitan heartbeat.

Home to over four million residents, Casablanca is where modern Morocco invents its future. Here, towering glass skyscrapers stand alongside grand French colonial Art Deco boulevards, avant-garde art foundations thrive in restored 1930s villas, and the monumental Hassan II Mosque juts out into the wild Atlantic surf like an imperial granite galleon. Whether you are savoring freshly shucked Dakhla oysters at the Marché Central, sipping a vintage cocktail at Rick’s Café, or wandering the tranquil olive markets of the Habous Quarter, Casablanca rewards curious travelers with urban sophistication found nowhere else in the kingdom.

The 24 to 48-Hour Casa Strategy:
Give Casablanca 1 to 2 full days at the beginning or end of your Moroccan circuit. It provides the perfect modern counterweight to the medieval sensory intensity of the imperial medinas, with high-speed Al Boraq train links delivering you to Tangier in just 2 hours and 10 minutes or Rabat in 50 minutes.

Top Sights & Architectural Marvels

Casablanca's landmarks span royal engineering triumphs, historic Andalusian neighborhoods, and one of the world's most concentrated collections of 20th-century Mauresque and Art Deco architecture.

1. Hassan II Mosque: An Oceanfront Feat of Craftsmanship

Completed in 1993, the Hassan II Mosque is one of the grandest religious monuments ever built and one of only two active mosques in Morocco open to non-Muslim visitors through guided tours.

  • Architectural Marvels: Designed by French architect Michel Pinseau, the mosque sits on a rocky outcrop projecting directly over the Atlantic, inspired by the Quranic verse stating that "the throne of God was built upon the water." Its 210-meter minaret—the second tallest in the world—is capped by a green laser pointing 30 kilometers toward Mecca.
  • The Interior: Built by more than 10,000 master artisans (maalems), the prayer hall accommodates 25,000 worshippers under a massive 1,100-ton titanium roof that glides open to the open sky in just three minutes. Features heated Italian travertine floors, polished titanium doors, hand-carved Atlas cedar ceilings, and subterranean marble ablution halls shaped like giant lotus flowers.
  • Visitor Logistics: Non-Muslim guided tours run several times daily outside prayer hours (typically 9:00 AM, 10:00 AM, 11:00 AM, and 2:00 PM; closed for tours on Friday mornings). Modest clothing is strictly enforced (knees and shoulders covered); shoe bags are provided at the entrance.

2. The Habous Quarter (Nouvelle Médina)

Conceived in the 1920s by French urban planner Albert Laprade to alleviate housing shortages, the Habous Quarter is an architectural triumph: a sanitized, picturesque reimagining of a traditional Moroccan medina complete with graceful stone arcades, manicured public plazas, and sunlit courtyards.

  • Mahkama du Pacha: A palatial 1940s Muslim courthouse featuring more than 60 opulent chambers richly adorned with intricate stuccowork, zellij mosaics, and carved cedar comparable to Granada's Alhambra. (Enter with a certified local guide or request polite permission from security guards when court is not in session).
  • Souk des Olives (Olive Market): A quaint walled courtyard packed with giant wooden barrels overflowing with dozens of varieties of Moroccan olives—cured in sea salt, marinated in fiery harissa, crushed with wild thyme, or stuffed with preserved lemon.
  • Pâtisserie Bennis Habous: Operating since 1930 inside a small tiled alleyway. Peer over the wooden counter to purchase the kingdom's finest cornes de gazelle (thin pastry filled with almond paste and orange blossom water), honey-soaked almond briouats, and savory pigeon pastilla.

3. Downtown Art Deco & Mauresque Walking Circuit

In the 1910s and 1920s, French resident-general Hubert Lyautey turned Casablanca into an avant-garde urban design laboratory. Leading architects fused French Art Nouveau and Art Deco with traditional Moroccan decorative elements, birthing the unique Neo-Mauresque style.

  • Place Mohammed V: Casa's grand administrative plaza, anchored by the iconic 1930s Wilaya clock tower, the grand Palais de Justice, and French colonial arcades.
  • Boulevard Mohammed V: Stroll along the central tram line past the ornate, weathered facades of the historic Hotel Lincoln and the 1930s Cinéma Rialto, where Josephine Baker and Édith Piaf once performed.
  • Villa des Arts: An exquisite 1934 Art Deco villa surrounded by serene gardens, hosting cutting-edge contemporary Moroccan painting, sculpture, and photography exhibitions.

Where to Eat: Atlantic Seafood & Iconic Salons

As Morocco’s commercial capital, Casablanca attracts the country’s top culinary talent. Dining ranges from rough-and-ready dockside oyster counters to high-end French brasseries.

1. Marché Central: The Ultimate Fishmonger Lunch

Located along Boulevard Mohammed V, this historic 1917 covered market is a feast for the senses. At its center stands a lively ring of fresh fishmonger stalls piled high with Atlantic sole, sea bass, red mullet, king prawns, and live rock oysters from Dakhla.

  • How to Dine: Select your raw seafood directly from the stall vendor by the kilo. Hand your fresh catch to one of the small open grill stalls in the outer ring. For a modest cooking fee (20–30 MAD), they will season it with spicy garlic chermoula, grill or fry it to golden perfection, and serve it alongside warm crusty baguettes, Moroccan salad, and fiery harissa dip.

2. Rick’s Café: Hollywood Legend Brought to Life

While Humphrey Bogart and Ingrid Bergman filmed the 1942 cinematic masterpiece entirely on a Warner Bros soundstage in Burbank, former American diplomat Kathy Kriger famously brought the fictional gin joint to life in 2004. Housed inside a restored 1930 riad tucked against the old medina walls, Rick’s Café features grand archways, brass chandeliers, curved balconies, and a live resident pianist playing "As Time Goes By".

  • Insider Tip: Reservations are essential for dinner. Order a classic French 75 cocktail or Casablanca beer at the mahogany bar and savor their grilled sea bass or lamb shank. Dress code is smart-casual (no shorts or flip-flops).

3. La Taverne du Dauphin & Corniche Dining

  • La Taverne du Dauphin: Operating since 1958 near Casa-Port station. Beloved by local business executives and artists for its unpretentious retro decor and superlative seafood: fried baby squid, sea urchins in season, and garlic-sautéed Atlantic gambas.
  • Le Cabestan Ocean View: Perched atop the sea cliffs along the Corniche near the El Hank Lighthouse. The premier sunset hotspot for Mediterranean-fusion gastronomy, creative cocktails, and panoramic floor-to-ceiling ocean vistas.

Where to Stay: Modern Elegance & Art Deco Heritage

Unlike imperial cities dominated by historic riads, Casablanca’s accommodations shine with sleek high-rise oceanfront resorts and converted mid-century boutique townhouses.

Handpicked Stays by Budget Category

  • Budget & Transit Stays ($40–$75/night):
    • Ibis Casablanca City Center: Located directly opposite Casa-Port railway station—ideal for travelers arriving via the high-speed Al Boraq train.
    • Onomo Hotel Casablanca City Center: Sleek, design-forward hotel in the lively Maarif district with modern Moroccan street art decor.
  • Mid-Range Boutique Character ($90–$180/night):
    • Hôtel & Spa Le Doge: A luxurious Relais & Châteaux gem housed inside an authentic 1930s Art Deco mansion in the diplomatic district. Each suite is dedicated to a prominent 1930s artist (Coco Chanel, Ernest Hemingway, Charlie Chaplin).
    • Radisson Blu Hotel Casablanca City Center: Modern luxury right on Boulevard Mohammed V with an impressive art collection, indoor pool, and panoramic skyline views.
  • Luxury & Oceanfront Resorts ($250–$600+/night):
    • Four Seasons Hotel Casablanca: Oceanfront sanctuary on the Anfa beachfront with private balconies overlooking the Atlantic breakers, private cabana pools, and French-Moroccan dining.

Practical City Navigation & Smart Transfers

Getting Around: Tramway vs. Red Petit Taxis

  • The Casablanca Tramway: Clean, air-conditioned, ultra-modern, and exceptionally safe. Two extensive lines connect Casa-Voyageurs station, downtown, Hassan II Mosque, and the Ain Diab beach promenade. A single rechargeable ticket costs just 8 MAD (under $1 USD).
  • Red Petit Taxis: Abundant across the city. By Moroccan law, drivers must turn on the digital taximeter (compteur). If a driver refuses or claims it is broken, decline the ride and hail the next cab. A 50% legal night surcharge applies after 8:00 PM.
  • Airport Transfers (CMN): Take the official ONCF airport train from the basement level of Terminal 1 directly into Casa-Port or Casa-Voyageurs stations. Runs every hour, takes only 35 minutes, and costs 50 MAD ($5 USD)—far faster and cheaper than highway airport taxis (300–400 MAD).

Best Quick Day Trips by High-Speed Rail

  • Rabat (50 minutes): Morocco's tranquil coastal capital. Ride the frequent express train to visit the cliffside Kasbah of the Udayas, Hassan Tower, and the Mohammed VI Museum of Modern Art.
  • El Jadida (1.5 hours): Explore the historic Portuguese fortified citadel of Mazagan and its haunting subterranean vaulted cistern, famously used in Orson Welles’ films.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many days do you need in Casablanca?

One full day (or 2 nights) is the sweet spot for most visitors. This allows ample time for an in-depth morning tour of Hassan II Mosque, an afternoon stroll through the Habous Quarter and Marché Central, and an atmospheric dinner at Rick's Café or along the oceanfront Corniche before boarding the high-speed train onward.

Is Casablanca safe for travelers?

Yes, Casablanca is generally very safe. Like any major global metropolis of 4 million people, exercise sensible street awareness: keep smartphones and cameras secure along crowded sidewalks, avoid walking unlit alleys in the ancient medina late at night, and always use metered red taxis or the modern tramway.

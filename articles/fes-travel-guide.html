Fes

Fes Travel Guide: Medina, Tanneries & Where to Stay (2026)

By Reda El Amrani & TravelMoveMorocco Team
Updated March 2026
11 min read (1,720 words)
[ Insert Featured Image: Chouara Leather Tannery Stone Vats & Medina Rooftops ]
The 11th-century honeycomb dyeing vats of Chouara Tannery, operating unchanged for a millennium.

If Marrakech is the theatrical heartbeat of Morocco, Fes is its spiritual, scholastic, and culinary soul. Founded in 789 AD by Idris I on the banks of the Jawhar River, this ancient imperial metropolis preserves the world's most complete medieval Islamic urban fabric.

Within the ancient perimeter walls of Fes el-Bali (Old Fes) lie over 9,400 interconnected alleyways, dead-end lanes (derbs), and vaulted tunnels where internal combustion engines have never set wheel. Mules laden with gas canisters, cedar trunks, and olive barrels still claim the right of way with the guttural cry of "Balak! Balak!" (Make way!). Entering Fes is not sightseeing; it is stepping directly into a living, working 14th-century civilization.

Medina Navigation Compass: Fes el-Bali is shaped like a giant natural bowl sloping downward toward the river. Two primary arteries lead downhill from the monumental western gate (Bab Bou Jeloud): Tala'a Kebira (The Big Slope) and Tala'a Seghira (The Little Slope). If you are walking downhill, you are heading into the core of the souks; if you walk steadily uphill, you will eventually reach the outer gates and road ring.

Top Attractions & Secret Local Sanctuaries

Exploring Fes requires patience and comfortable footwear with non-slip soles. Here are the essential monuments and the tranquil retreats that provide refuge from the sensory intensity of the souks.

1. Chouara Tannery: A Thousand Years of Ancient Craft

Dating back to the 11th century, Chouara is the largest of the three historic leather tanneries operating in Fes. Cow, sheep, goat, and camel hides are first soaked in massive white stone vats containing a pungent blend of limestone, water, and pigeon droppings (rich in natural ammonia that tenderizes the tough skins). Men stomp the hides waist-deep for hours before transferring them into round earthen dyeing pots filled with natural pigments: crushed poppy flowers for red, saffron and turmeric for yellow, indigo for blue, and cedar wood for brown.

  • When to Visit: Arrive between 9:00 AM and 11:30 AM when the morning sun illuminates the vats and tanners actively work the dyes. Midday heat amplifies the scent and the workers take extended lunch breaks.
  • Terrace Etiquette: You view the tanneries from the private viewing terraces of surrounding leather goods shops (shops #10, #30, and #64 offer prime panoramic angles). Shop assistants will hand you a sprig of fresh mint to hold under your nose. You are under no obligation to purchase a jacket or pouf; if you do not buy, leave a customary 15–20 MAD tip per person for the terrace host.

2. Al-Qarawiyyin: The World's Oldest University

Founded in 859 AD by a wealthy refugee woman from Kairouan named Fatima al-Fihri, the University of al-Qarawiyyin is recognized by UNESCO and the Guinness World Records as the oldest continuously operating higher educational institution in the world—centuries older than Oxford or Bologna. Celebrated historical scholars such as Ibn Khaldun, Al-Idrisi, and Maimonides walked these tiled halls.

  • Visiting Note: While non-Muslims cannot enter the sacred prayer hall, you can admire the marble fountains, Andalusian-style horseshoe arches, and hand-carved cedar arcades through its magnificent bronze-studded gates (the gates off Place Seffarine and Souk el-Attarine offer stunning sightlines).
  • The Restored Library: The university library, housing invaluable 9th-century illuminated Qurans on gazelle parchment, was recently restored by architect Aziza Chaouni and is accessible for cultural exhibitions.

3. Bou Inania Madrasa & Al-Attarine Madrasa

Built in the 14th century under the Marinid dynasty, these two theological colleges represent the undisputed peak of Islamic ornamentation in North Africa.

  • Medersa Bou Inania (Bab Bou Jeloud): The only madrasa in Fes that also functioned as a congregational Friday mosque with its own green-tiled minaret. Marvel at the intricate cedar screen (mashrabiya), carved green plaster bands, and the tranquil stream running through its courtyard.
  • Medersa el-Attarine (Spice Souk): Built by Sultan Abu Sa'id Uthman II right in the heart of the perfume and spice quarter. Its central marble basin, miniature scale, and luminous floral zellij mosaics make it one of the most harmonious architectural spaces on earth.

4. Place Seffarine: The Sound of Medieval Metallurgy

Tucked beneath towering centuries-old plane trees, this small, sunlit square rings with the rhythmic clatter of brass and coppersmiths. Master artisans hammer enormous wedding tagines, copper cauldrons, water vessels, and samovars by hand using charcoal embers. It is one of the medina's most cinematic corners.

5. The Jewish Mellah & Ibn Danan Synagogue

Located just outside the old medina walls in Fes el-Jdid, the Mellah was established in the 15th century. Its architecture stands in sharp contrast to the introverted Islamic medina: houses feature prominent wooden windows, carved exterior balconies, and open wrought-iron facades facing the street. Visit the meticulously restored 17th-century Ibn Danan Synagogue and the poignant, sun-bleached tombstones of the Jewish Cemetery.

Where to Eat: The Epicenter of Fassi Gastronomy

Moroccans universally revere Fes as the gastronomic capital of the kingdom. While Marrakech specializes in bold, rustic fire-cooked meats, Fassi cuisine is aristocratic, subtle, and sophisticated—defined by the delicate interplay of sweet and savory spices, almond pastes, orange blossom waters, and slow braising.

Signature Fassi Dishes You Must Try

  • Pastilla Fassi (Bastilla): The quintessential dish of Fes. Layers of featherweight pastry (warqa) encase tender spiced pigeon or chicken braised with cinnamon, saffron, and eggs, topped with toasted crushed almonds, powdered sugar, and criss-cross cinnamon lines.
  • Khlii: An ancestral Fassi specialty of sun-dried beef marinated in garlic, cumin, and coriander, preserved in melted animal fat. Ordered at street stalls scrambled into farm-fresh eggs with a splash of olive oil and hot bread.
  • Rfissa: Shredded paper-thin flatbread (trid) soaked in a fragrant, golden broth of chicken, lentils, caramelized onions, and pungent fenugreek seeds (helba). Pure comfort food.

Top Authentic Restaurants & Courtyards

  • The Ruined Garden (R'cif / Bab Guissa): Set inside the romantic overgrown ruins of a merchant’s riad. Chef-created Moroccan mezze lunches (roasted beet salad with orange, sardine tagines, slow-cooked beef with quince) in a verdant, bird-filled garden sanctuary. Dinner requires booking 24 hours in advance for slow-cooked specialties.
  • Café Clock (Tala'a Kebira): A vibrant cultural café spread across three restored stories near the Bou Inania Water Clock. Famous for its tender camel burger with spiced spiced wedges, fresh mint lemonade, and weekly evening storytelling sessions with master Fassi storytellers.
  • Dar Roumana (Ziat): Housed in a pristine riad covered in original floor-to-ceiling tilework. French-trained chefs craft an unforgettable Mediterranean-Moroccan market tasting menu that changes nightly based on what is freshest in the souk. Reservations essential.
  • Chez Thami (Bab Bou Jeloud): A humble, four-table open-air eatery right at the medina entrance. Thami has been serving rich lamb and prune tagines, vegetable couscous, and fried aubergines with garlic for decades at unbeatable local prices.

Where to Stay: Authentic Riads by Budget

Staying inside Fes el-Bali is non-negotiable if you want to experience the true soul of the city. Waking up to the distant dawn adhan, eating freshly baked msemen on a riad rooftop as the morning mist lifts over the Merenid Tombs, and having quiet tea beside a tiled courtyard fountain is unmatched.

Handpicked Riads by Budget Category

  • Budget Travelers & Solos ($35–$75/night):
    • Riad Verus: A boutique traveler hub with a vibrant interior courtyard, pod-style luxury dorms and private rooms, weekly family dinners, and an active social calendar.
    • Dar Seffarine: A 600-year-old architectural marvel lovingly restored by an Iraqi-Moroccan architect and his Norwegian wife. Exceptional breakfast included on one of the highest terraces in the city.
  • Mid-Range Character & Charm ($90–$220/night):
    • Riad Laaroussa: A lavish 17th-century palace built around an orange tree courtyard. Features an authentic underground 300-year-old hammam, heated pool, and unmatched customer service.
    • Dar El Yasmine: Exceptionally hospitable family-run riad near Bab Bou Jeloud with spotless rooms, antique furnishings, and warm local advice.
    • Riad Maison Bleue: Historic former residence of the royal astrologer with three romantic courtyards, library, and fine dining.
  • Luxury & Palace Living ($280–$850+/night):
    • Palais Amani: An opulent 18-bedroom Art Deco palace situated near Bab Ftouh. Boasts lush citrus gardens, cooking workshops, an exemplary private hammam, and rooftop cocktail lounge.
    • Karawan Riad: A refined restoration of a former 17th-century harem and trade mansion, featuring custom Italian linens, curated antiques, and peaceful suite retreats.
    • Hotel Sahrai: For those preferring contemporary design outside the maze: located on a hill in Ville Nouvelle with infinity pool, Givenchy spa, and panoramic views over the entire medina.

Practical Insider Tips & Street Smarts

Because Fes is older and more culturally conservative than Marrakech, travelers who navigate the medina with respect and basic street-savvy will be rewarded with remarkable warmth.

The "Faux Guide" Phenomenon & How to Handle It

Fes is renowned for persistent unofficial guides lounging at Bab Bou Jeloud, parking lots, and alleyway choke points. They will often approach saying: "The tannery is on strike / closed for prayer," "That street is a private dead-end," or "I am a student simply practicing my English."

  • The Strategy: Never break your stride. Politely decline with a calm, friendly smile and a firm "La, Shukran" (No, thank you) or "Je connais le chemin" (I know the way).
  • If Truly Lost: Do not ask idle young men on corners. Instead, step inside a brick-and-mortar grocery, bakery, or pharmacy and ask the shopkeeper: "Feen Bab Bou Jeloud?" (Where is the Blue Gate?). They will gladly point you in the right direction without expecting payment.
  • Street Sign Decoder: Look up at the building corners for ceramic street plaques:
    • Hexagonal tile plaque: Indicates a continuous through-street leading to an exit or gate.
    • Octagonal / square tile plaque: Indicates a blind alley or dead-end (cul-de-sac).

Best Time to Visit Fes

  • Spring (April to May): Perfect weather with wildflowers across the surrounding hillsides and pleasant 22°C–26°C (72°F–79°F) daytime temperatures.
  • Autumn (September to November): Fresh harvest season for figs, dates, and olives, with crisp comfortable walking conditions.
  • Summer Caution (July to August): Temperatures inside the deep valley basin regularly cross 40°C–44°C (104°F–111°F). The stone walls radiate heat late into the evening.

Best Day Trips from Fes

1. Volubilis (Walili) & Moulay Idriss Zerhoun

Just 75 minutes west of Fes sits Volubilis, Morocco’s premier UNESCO Roman archaeological site. Dating to the 3rd century BC, this ancient outpost exported olive oil and wild animals across the Roman Empire. Wander among remarkably preserved in-situ floor mosaics depicting Diana bathing and the labors of Hercules, the Basilica, and the Arch of Caracalla. Pair this with a stroll through the sacred hilltop town of Moulay Idriss Zerhoun, where Idris I brought Islam to Morocco.

2. Imperial Meknes

A quick 35-minute train ride brings you to Meknes, the ambitious 17th-century capital created by Sultan Moulay Ismail. Admire the colossal gate of Bab el-Mansour, the royal granaries and stables of Heri es-Souani (designed to house 12,000 royal horses), and the atmospheric medina souks.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many days do you need in Fes?

Two full days allows you to thoroughly explore the medina monuments, tanneries, artisan quarters, and Jewish Mellah without rushing. Add a third day if you plan to visit the Roman ruins of Volubilis and Meknes.

Is Fes safe for solo female travelers?

Yes, violent crime is practically non-existent. However, verbal catcalling and unsolicited navigation offers can occur in quieter alleys. Dressing modestly (covering shoulders, collarbones, and knees), wearing sunglasses to deflect direct eye contact in crowded souks, and walking with purpose ensures a hassle-free visit.

Should I hire a licensed guide for the medina?

For your very first morning, hiring an official badge-carrying guide through your riad or the official tourist office (roughly 350–500 MAD for a half day) is highly recommended. A good guide will unlock private courtyards, decipher complex historical dynasties, and shield you from street touts while you acclimate to the medina's scale.
